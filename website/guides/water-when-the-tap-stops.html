Water When the Tap Stops is for the hour the tap runs dry, and for the days you may have to drink, cook and wash without it. If the water has just stopped, it uses the pressure still left in the pipes: drinking bottles first, then the bath for flushing and washing, the heater switched off, and the toilet left unflushed so the cistern stays a reserve. If the water is running but may be unsafe, it takes you straight to a check and a treatment card. The rule throughout is simple. If you are not sure, treat the water before you drink it, brush your teeth, wash food or make up baby formula, and boiling is the method it trusts first.

The rest of the book is how to be ready. It explains why a tap fails, from a building pump in a power cut to a burst main, a flood, a drought or an earthquake, and it keeps drinking water separate from the much larger amount you need to flush and wash. On 28 April 2025, water kept flowing in many places, but upper floors went dry when the pumps stopped, and the households that had filled bottles and a bath in the first hour hardly noticed. Using the Silva family in a fourth-floor flat in Lisbon, it works out a stock for the European Union’s 72 hours, about 12 litres a person, and shows how to stretch that to a week with containers, labels, rotation, chlorine, filters, cloudy water, water from outside and rain. You finish knowing what to store, how to treat what you have, and how to keep the household clean until the tap is safe again.
